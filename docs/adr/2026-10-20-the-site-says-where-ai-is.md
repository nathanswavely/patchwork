# The site says where AI is

Date: 2026-10-20. Status: **accepted**; implemented; **amended
2026-10-28** (decisions 5 and 6, see the end). Built: the page at `/ai`, both
parts, and the tests behind decisions 3 and 6, then on 2026-10-29 the links
from About and the Label and the disclosure paragraph, rendered from the
paired ADR's switch (`{ai_disclosure}` in `internal/handler/ai_defaults.go`).
Builds on docs/adr/028 (shipped
legal text with an admin override, every claim checked against the code),
docs/adr/037 (project-owned text that an instance cannot override), and
docs/adr/2026-09-18-counting-visitors-without-watching-anyone.md (a policy
paragraph rendered from the switch it describes). Paired with
docs/adr/2026-10-20-a-post-says-whether-ai-made-it.md, which this page
explains to visitors.

## Context

In the autumn of 2026 the reference instance's town filled with event
listings and newsletters written by language models and presented as if a
person had written them. A visitor arriving at a community site now has a
reasonable first question, which is whether this is another one. Patchwork
has no page that answers it.

The answer is not simple, and a simple one would be false. Three things are
true at once:

- **Nothing on the running site calls a model.** No handler, worker or page
  sends anything to an AI service. Nothing a person posts is summarized,
  ranked, rewritten, moderated or generated by one. The default terms already
  say nothing ranks what people see and that bots do not get accounts.
- **AI crawlers are refused.** `internal/middleware/aicrawler.go` opts out of
  them by name in robots.txt and answers the ones that ignore it with a 403.
  Neither layer can stop a scraper that lies about its user agent, and copies
  sent by federation live on other servers under their rules.
- **The software is built with a great deal of AI help.** Most commits are
  co-authored by a model. The copy ledger (`copy/ledger.json`) records, for
  every visitor-readable string, whether a person wrote it (`human`), a model
  drafted it and a person chose to keep it (`ai-fine`), or nobody has looked
  yet (`unreviewed`). On this date, of 434 paragraph-length strings (14,482
  words), 44% of the words were written by a person, 55% drafted and kept,
  and 1% unreviewed. Nearly all the unreviewed strings are labels and helper
  lines: 496 of the 503.

That last fact is why a single percentage of strings is the wrong number to
publish. It counts "Save" the same as a paragraph of the privacy policy,
understates how much of the actual writing a person did, and implies that a
button has an author in the sense a policy about prose cares about.

A page that said "no AI here" would be the thing visitors are trying to tell
apart from us: a reassuring claim nobody can check. The distinction worth
offering is precision. Say exactly where AI is and is not, and point at what
holds each sentence true.

## Decision

**1. One public page at `/ai`, answering three questions.** Linked from the
footer beside Privacy and Terms, from one sentence on About, and from the
Label. No banner and no badge on other pages: a loud "made by humans" mark
is what the sites we are distinguishing ourselves from would put up.

- *Does AI touch you or what you post here?* No, and what that covers.
- *Can AI companies take what is here?* We refuse them by name, and here is
  where that stops working.
- *Was this built with AI?* Yes. Who designs it, who decides, who reviews,
  and the ledger's numbers.

**2. The page has two parts, owned by two parties.** The *project* part
describes the software. It ships in the binary and an instance cannot edit
it, for the same reason docs/adr/037 pins the lining: a deployment that could
keep the page and change the facts would turn a disclosure into decoration.
A fork that wants different text forks the repository, and the tests below
travel with it. The *stewards'* part describes what the people running this
quilt do and what they ask of members. It is edited from the admin panel
beside the legal documents and renders under a heading naming the quilt. It
is where an instance's own content policy lives, including the reference
instance's (labelled AI content is welcome, unlabelled is not).

**3. Every claim in the project part is rendered from, or tested against,
the thing it describes.** The same discipline as docs/adr/028, made
mechanical where it can be:

- the crawler list is rendered from `aiCrawlerUATokens` and the robots.txt
  groups, so the page cannot name a crawler the middleware does not refuse;
- the copy figures (decision 5) are computed at build time from
  `copy/ledger.json` and embedded, so they move as review happens and never
  need hand-editing;
- the disclosure paragraph is rendered from the instance switch in the paired
  ADR, as `{usage_stats}` is today;
- "nothing calls a model" is held by a test that fails if `go.mod` or
  `web/package.json` gains a known AI SDK, and by an allowlist of the
  packages that make outbound requests (federation, event sources, mail),
  which the page lists. A new outbound caller fails the build until the page
  says what it talks to.

**4. The project part says what the build used, plainly.** Which tools wrote
code and copy, that one maintainer designs the system and makes every
governance call, that the decision records in `docs/adr/` were drafted with
AI help and left that way, and what `human`, `ai-fine` and `unreviewed` mean
in the ledger. It does not apologize and does not describe other sites.

**5. Copy is reported in two classes, and the pages a visitor reads are
named.** The ledger gains a derived `class` for each string, read by both
`make copy-report` and the page:

- **Interface text**: buttons, labels, field hints, placeholders, aria
  labels, tooltips and API error bodies. Classified by the string's `kind`
  first and its length tier second, so a 16-word error message is interface
  text and a 14-word sentence on About is not. The page's claim for this
  class is that it was mostly drafted with AI and checked by a person for
  accuracy, and it reports reviewed against not yet reviewed. Authorship is
  not a meaningful question for a button, so the page does not ask it.
- **Writing**: paragraphs, documents and explanations. Reported in words, as
  written by a person against drafted with AI and kept, with unreviewed
  stated separately. This is the figure the reference instance's content
  policy is about.

Beneath the two figures, a table names each page a visitor reads (the
lining, the charter templates, About, the Label, Privacy, Terms, this page)
with its writing split the same way. "This page was written by a person" is
easier to hold than a percentage, and a visitor can check it against the page
in front of them. Which source files make up a visitor page is a short list
kept beside the ledger. Admin-only screens count toward the totals and get no
row, since no visitor sees them.

On this date the table shows About as drafted entirely with AI and kept, and
Privacy and Terms as more drafted than written. The page publishes that as it
stands. It is labelled, which is what the policy asks.

**6. The page's own words are written by a person.** Its strings are
`human` in the ledger before it ships, and a test reads the ledger to keep it
so. A disclosure page drafted by a model and left unreviewed would break the
policy it states.

## Consequences

- Visitors get an answer they can check, including the parts that do not
  flatter the project.
- Adding an outbound integration or an AI dependency now means editing this
  page in the same change, which is the point.
- The ledger numbers are public. Unreviewed copy becomes visible debt rather
  than private debt, and the per-page table makes the order of work obvious:
  the visitor pages with the most drafted writing, starting with About.
- The ledger tool gains a `class` derivation and a visitor-page list, and
  `copy/REPORT.md` reports by class rather than by length tier alone.
- Instances that want a stricter or looser content policy write it in the
  stewards' part. The project part reads the same everywhere.

## Not decided here

- Whether the project part federates or appears in the instance actor.
- A per-patch version of the page. The patch-level declaration in the paired
  ADR covers the case that prompted the question.

## Amendment, 2026-10-28: the page states, the repository counts

Decision 5 is withdrawn as far as the page goes. The page no longer
publishes the copy ledger's figures or the per-page table. It says that AI
helped build the software, names the writing a person did (the lining, the
Label, the pages that explain what Patchwork is), says that much of the rest
was drafted with AI and reviewed, and points to `copy/REPORT.md` in the
public repository for the string-by-string record.

The reason is the reader. The figures were accurate, but a visitor deciding
whether to trust a community site does not need a ledger, and a table of
percentages invited them to read "53% drafted with AI" as a verdict on the
whole place, including on writing that is drafted because it is a template.
The record still exists and is still public; the page stops reciting it.

What this changes:

- The ledger is no longer embedded in the binary (`copy/embed.go` and
  `internal/copystats` are removed, 2.2 MB lighter).
- The sentence naming what a person wrote is the one claim on the page that
  is not mechanical. The ledger holds it instead: a page it names must be
  recorded as a person's, and where the ledger disagrees, the ledger is
  corrected in whichever direction is true. On this date it disagreed
  twice. About was recorded as drafted though it is the author's rewrite
  (03fc362 chose the understatement on purpose), and the charter templates
  were recorded as a person's though they are largely drafted.
- The two-class reporting of decision 5 survives where it belongs, in
  SlopChop's report, which anyone can regenerate from the repository.

Decision 6 is amended the same day. It required every string unique to the
page to be a person's. It now requires every one to be *decided*: a
person's words, or AI wording a person read and chose to keep. That is
exactly what SlopChop's copy-check already enforces for every string in
scope, so no test of the page's own repeats it. The stricter rule needed
one only because copy-check cannot tell a person's words from AI wording a
person kept. The site's policy is transparency,
not abstinence, and a disclosure page that is partly drafted is consistent
with it. The page carries no notice about itself: its account of the
site's writing already says much of the copy was drafted with AI and
reviewed, and the copy ledger records which strings, this page's included.
What the page must never do is ship unread, or claim a person wrote what
they did not.

Decisions 1 to 4 stand.
